# Powerlifting and the Conjugate Method

## What is Powerlifting?

Powerlifting is a strength sport with three competition lifts:

- **Squat** — bar on your back, bend your knees until your hip crease drops below the top of your knee, stand back up
- **Bench press** — lie on a bench, lower a bar to your chest, press it back up
- **Deadlift** — pull a bar off the floor until you're standing upright with it

In competition, each athlete gets three attempts at each lift. Your best successful attempt on each lift is added together to produce your **total**. Higher total wins. Lifters are grouped into weight classes and age categories so you're competing against people of similar size and life stage.

The number that defines your strength on any given lift is your **one-rep max (1RM)** — the most weight you can lift for a single repetition. Most training doesn't happen at your true 1RM (that's very taxing and risky), so lifters use formulas to estimate 1RM from sets done with lighter weight and more reps.

## What is the Conjugate Method?

Most beginner strength programs are **linear** — you add a little weight every session or every week, and your strength increases in a straight line until you stall out. This works great early on but stops working once you're more advanced, because you can't recover fast enough to keep setting records that frequently.

The **Conjugate Method** is a training system developed by Louie Simmons at Westside Barbell, adapted from Soviet and Eastern European sports science. Instead of doing the same movements over and over with slightly more weight each time, it rotates exercises constantly and trains multiple physical qualities at once.

### The Two Pillars

Conjugate training is built around two types of training days, each repeated once a week per movement pattern (upper body / lower body):

**Max Effort (ME)**
You work up to a true maximum — a 1, 2, or 3-rep max — on a _variation_ of the competition lift. Maybe it's a squat with the bar positioned differently, or a deadlift from an elevated surface, or a bench press with a different grip width. Hitting near-maximal weights trains your nervous system and raw strength. Rotating the exercise every 1–3 weeks prevents adaptation and keeps the training stimulus fresh.

**Dynamic Effort (DE)**
You lift a moderate weight (typically 50–70% of your max) for many sets of few reps (a common prescription is 8–12 sets of 2–3 reps), moving the bar as fast as possible. This trains **rate of force development** — how quickly you can recruit your muscles — which is distinct from absolute strength. Speed work also accumulates a lot of practice reps on the movement pattern without the recovery cost of going heavy.

### Accommodating Resistance

Conjugate programs often use **bands and chains** attached to the bar. These add resistance that increases as you move the bar through the lift (bands stretch more, chains lift off the floor). This is called _accommodating resistance_ and it addresses the fact that most lifts are easier in some positions than others — bands and chains make the easy parts harder so you're challenged throughout the full range of motion.

### Why the Constant Rotation?

Lifters adapt to specific exercises quickly. If you squat the same way every week, your body gets very efficient at that exact pattern and stops making strength gains from it. By rotating exercises — using different bars, different stances, different ranges of motion — you keep presenting a new stimulus while still training the same underlying muscles and movement patterns. The competition lifts themselves are practiced enough to maintain proficiency, but not so often that they become stale.

Because the Conjugate Method involves so many different exercises, it can be hard to remember when you last did something or what weights you've hit before. The DYEL Visualizer lets you load your training spreadsheet and quickly look up:

- When you last did a particular exercise
